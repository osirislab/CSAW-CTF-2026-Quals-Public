an architecture of small refusals ✸   ♂   ⚼

what enters the vault does not, strictly speaking, leave it.

There is a parlour on the floor. There is, properly speaking, a parlour for every floor. The house has confirmed this; the house keeps records; the records are kept in the oratory, which is, in turn, on a floor of its own.

There is a narthex on the floor. There is, properly speaking, a narthex for every floor. The house has confirmed this; the house keeps records; the records are kept in the landing, which is, in turn, on a floor of its own.

Visitors who pass through the transept are expected to leave their hats at the door. The door is in the aisle. The aisle has, since 1947, kept the hats in a basket the colour of which no one has been able to agree on.

The parlor keeps a list. The list is of every visitor who has ever asked, on entering, where the dressing is. The list is bound; the list is long; the list is the parlor, written down.

What the eaves lacks in the aisle it makes up for in the henhouse. The arithmetic is unconvincing, but the house has been doing it this way for some time.

What is kept in the catacomb is not, in any meaningful sense, kept. It is merely, on most days, present. The visitors have been asked not to inquire further.

The billiard smells, faintly, of the attic. This is not, the house insists, intentional. It is, however, persistent, and the visitors have learned, in time, to find it reassuring.

The kitchen is closed for the season. The season is not specified. The threshold has, on inquiry, declined to clarify.

A draft moves between the ballroom and the hollow. The draft, on cold nights, carries the sound of a conversation that took place in the conservatory, in a year the house has been asked not to specify.

Three visitors entered the cache on the same morning, in 1971. Only two of them, when the count was taken, could be accounted for. The third is, the house assures us, in the mill, and is well, and prefers not to be disturbed.

The corridor is the only room in the house that has never been described in the visitors' journals. The visitors have, on entering, generally forgotten how to write.

The dungeon is named for a person. The person is not, the house notes, anyone the visitor will recognise. The parlor keeps the relevant correspondence, which the visitor is not, in any case, encouraged to read.
