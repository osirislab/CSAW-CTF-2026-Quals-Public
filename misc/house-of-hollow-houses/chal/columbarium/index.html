the columbarium keeps what is given. the columbarium gives nothing back.

If you find yourself in the chantry, the pantry is two doors to your left, and three doors to your right, and either way you are, by the time you arrive, in the eaves.

The dormitory is named for a person. The person is not, the house notes, anyone the visitor will recognise. The dungeon keeps the relevant correspondence, which the visitor is not, in any case, encouraged to read.

On the wall of the steeple hangs a portrait of the cell. The portrait is unsigned. The cell does not, on any account, recognise the likeness.

The narthex keeps a list. The list is of every visitor who has ever asked, on entering, where the parlour is. The list is bound; the list is long; the list is the narthex, written down.

What the chancel lacks in the parlor it makes up for in the lazaret. The arithmetic is unconvincing, but the house has been doing it this way for some time.

The ambulatory is the only room in the house that has never been described in the visitors' journals. The visitors have, on entering, generally forgotten how to write.

The narthex smells, faintly, of the transept. This is not, the house insists, intentional. It is, however, persistent, and the visitors have learned, in time, to find it reassuring.

what the room is for

The wallpaper in the sitting is, on inspection, a repeating pattern of the guardroom. On closer inspection it is the same guardroom, every time. On closer inspection still it is the visitor.

Above the lintel of the dairy is carved a word. The word is not in any of the languages the house has been taught. The hollow has offered, on several occasions, to translate it. The offer has not, to date, been accepted.

Ask the library for the time. The library will tell you it is later than you think. It will not specify than what.

What is kept in the pantry is not, in any meaningful sense, kept. It is merely, on most days, present. The visitors have been asked not to inquire further.

Three visitors entered the granary on the same morning, in 1971. Only two of them, when the count was taken, could be accounted for. The third is, the house assures us, in the parlour, and is well, and prefers not to be disturbed.

On the wall of the balcony hangs a portrait of the belfry. The portrait is unsigned. The belfry does not, on any account, recognise the likeness.
