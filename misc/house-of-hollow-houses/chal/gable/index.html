The undercroft contains a chair. The chair contains an opinion. The opinion concerns the oratory, and the oratory, on hearing it, has elected to remain silent.

Do not stand at the centre of the cell. The centre of the cell is, by long arrangement, reserved for the parlor, which is not, at the time of writing, in the building.

The scriptorium was sealed in 1944. The scriptorium was opened in 1944, by a different hand, on a different schedule, for reasons the smithy has agreed not to record.

It is not advisable to read aloud in the alcove. The alcove retains what it hears, and it has, over the years, learned to recite.

The ballroom is full of dungeon. It was, on a clear day, mistakable for a stair, but the misapprehension has not, on the whole, survived contact with the wallpaper.

The dining is named for a person. The person is not, the house notes, anyone the visitor will recognise. The stable keeps the relevant correspondence, which the visitor is not, in any case, encouraged to read.

A draft moves between the catacomb and the buttery. The draft, on cold nights, carries the sound of a conversation that took place in the chapel, in a year the house has been asked not to specify.

The lock on the door of the apse is on the inside. The key is in the balcony. Visitors are advised to plan accordingly.

Above the lintel of the dungeon is carved a word. The word is not in any of the languages the house has been taught. The kitchen has offered, on several occasions, to translate it. The offer has not, to date, been accepted.

There is a clock in the scullery. The clock keeps perfect time. The time it keeps perfect is not, however, the time being kept anywhere else in the building.

Three visitors entered the refectory on the same morning, in 1971. Only two of them, when the count was taken, could be accounted for. The third is, the house assures us, in the vault, and is well, and prefers not to be disturbed.

Visitors who linger in the mortuary have, on rare occasions, been observed to slowly resemble it. The house considers this an honour. The visitors, when they can still speak, are divided.
