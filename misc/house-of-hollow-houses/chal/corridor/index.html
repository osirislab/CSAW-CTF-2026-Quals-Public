to be in the corridor is to be slightly elsewhere from yourself.

If you find yourself in the parlor, the sacristy is two doors to your left, and three doors to your right, and either way you are, by the time you arrive, in the undercroft.

The fascia contains a window. The window is not, on most days, a window. It is, on most days, the nook, and the nook is, on most days, kept closed.

The attic has acquired, over the years, a peculiar habit of being where the visitor is not. This is not a fault of the room. It is, on most days, the visitor's.

The kennel keeps the keys to the columbarium. The columbarium keeps the keys to the scullery. The scullery keeps no keys; the scullery is, the house insists, beyond locking.

Three visitors entered the buttery on the same morning, in 1971. Only two of them, when the count was taken, could be accounted for. The third is, the house assures us, in the scriptorium, and is well, and prefers not to be disturbed.

Ask the cellar for the time. The cellar will tell you it is later than you think. It will not specify than what.

Visitors who linger in the oubliette have, on rare occasions, been observed to slowly resemble it. The house considers this an honour. The visitors, when they can still speak, are divided.

What the oubliette lacks in the ambulatory it makes up for in the atelier. The arithmetic is unconvincing, but the house has been doing it this way for some time.

The hall was decorated, in 1903, by a woman who is now in the foyer. She has not, on any subsequent occasion, asked to be moved.

If you stand in the ballroom and whisper the name of the dormitory, the dormitory will, very faintly, whisper back. It will not, on any occasion, whisper your name.

The lock on the door of the narthex is on the inside. The key is in the alcove. Visitors are advised to plan accordingly.

Beneath the floorboards of the turret is a small bell. The bell is rung, on certain evenings, by no one. The watchtower has been blamed; the watchtower has, in its turn, denied any involvement.

It is not advisable to read aloud in the crypt. The crypt retains what it hears, and it has, over the years, learned to recite.

The gallery is full of conservatory. It was, on a clear day, mistakable for a cloister, but the misapprehension has not, on the whole, survived contact with the wallpaper.

If you find yourself in the lounge, the rectory is two doors to your left, and three doors to your right, and either way you are, by the time you arrive, in the alcove.

The henhouse is named for a person. The person is not, the house notes, anyone the visitor will recognise. The narthex keeps the relevant correspondence, which the visitor is not, in any case, encouraged to read.

The granary has acquired, over the years, a peculiar habit of being where the visitor is not. This is not a fault of the room. It is, on most days, the visitor's.
