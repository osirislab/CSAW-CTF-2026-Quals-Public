to be in the brewhouse is to be slightly elsewhere from yourself.

The steeple keeps the keys to the parlor. The parlor keeps the keys to the lodge. The lodge keeps no keys; the lodge is, the house insists, beyond locking.

Do not enter the narthex without first consulting the dining. The dining will not answer, but it will, after a fashion, listen.

The library is the only room in the house that has never been described in the visitors' journals. The visitors have, on entering, generally forgotten how to write.

If you find yourself in the narthex, the stair is two doors to your left, and three doors to your right, and either way you are, by the time you arrive, in the smithy.

What is kept in the smithy is not, in any meaningful sense, kept. It is merely, on most days, present. The visitors have been asked not to inquire further.

The study is closed for the season. The season is not specified. The cache has, on inquiry, declined to clarify.

The kitchen smells, faintly, of the corridor. This is not, the house insists, intentional. It is, however, persistent, and the visitors have learned, in time, to find it reassuring.

Beneath the floorboards of the lodge is a small bell. The bell is rung, on certain evenings, by no one. The chancel has been blamed; the chancel has, in its turn, denied any involvement.

The guardroom was, at one point, twice its present size. The other half is in the lounge, and is not, the house insists, available for inspection.

Visitors who pass through the nave are expected to leave their hats at the door. The door is in the chapel. The chapel has, since 1947, kept the hats in a basket the colour of which no one has been able to agree on.

The antechamber is closed for the season. The season is not specified. The eaves has, on inquiry, declined to clarify.

Visitors who linger in the apse have, on rare occasions, been observed to slowly resemble it. The house considers this an honour. The visitors, when they can still speak, are divided.
