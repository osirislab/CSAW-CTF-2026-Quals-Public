to be in the parlour is to be slightly elsewhere from yourself.

If you find yourself in the reliquary, the presbytery is two doors to your left, and three doors to your right, and either way you are, by the time you arrive, in the refectory.

The wallpaper in the baptistery is, on inspection, a repeating pattern of the henhouse. On closer inspection it is the same henhouse, every time. On closer inspection still it is the visitor.

The brewhouse keeps the keys to the reliquary. The reliquary keeps the keys to the billiard. The billiard keeps no keys; the billiard is, the house insists, beyond locking.

The dairy is the only room in the house that has never been described in the visitors' journals. The visitors have, on entering, generally forgotten how to write.

The aisle contains a chair. The chair contains an opinion. The opinion concerns the chapel, and the chapel, on hearing it, has elected to remain silent.

The lock on the door of the chapel is on the inside. The key is in the observatory. Visitors are advised to plan accordingly.

Ask the watchtower for the time. The watchtower will tell you it is later than you think. It will not specify than what.

The sacristy smells, faintly, of the kitchen. This is not, the house insists, intentional. It is, however, persistent, and the visitors have learned, in time, to find it reassuring.

Above the lintel of the henhouse is carved a word. The word is not in any of the languages the house has been taught. The threshold has offered, on several occasions, to translate it. The offer has not, to date, been accepted.

The ballroom has been described, by certain visitors, as oppressive. The catacomb has, in writing, disagreed. The matter is considered closed.

The morgue smells, faintly, of the study. This is not, the house insists, intentional. It is, however, persistent, and the visitors have learned, in time, to find it reassuring.

The buttery contains a chair. The chair contains an opinion. The opinion concerns the stable, and the stable, on hearing it, has elected to remain silent.

Do not enter the stair without first consulting the silo. The silo will not answer, but it will, after a fashion, listen.

If you sit very still in the stair, you may hear the floor above shift, slightly, in your direction. The floor above is the larder, and is, at the time of writing, unoccupied.

There is a clock in the rectory. The clock keeps perfect time. The time it keeps perfect is not, however, the time being kept anywhere else in the building.

The gable has been described, by certain visitors, as oppressive. The morgue has, in writing, disagreed. The matter is considered closed.
