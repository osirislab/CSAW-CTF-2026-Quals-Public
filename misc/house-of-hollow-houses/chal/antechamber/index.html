a antechamber is a room which has been asked to apologise for itself.

If you stand in the nave and whisper the name of the tower, the tower will, very faintly, whisper back. It will not, on any occasion, whisper your name.

The landing was, at one point, twice its present size. The other half is in the oratory, and is not, the house insists, available for inspection.

The steeple has acquired, over the years, a peculiar habit of being where the visitor is not. This is not a fault of the room. It is, on most days, the visitor's.

The oubliette has been described, by certain visitors, as oppressive. The steeple has, in writing, disagreed. The matter is considered closed.

The chancel was decorated, in 1903, by a woman who is now in the lazaret. She has not, on any subsequent occasion, asked to be moved.

There is a cloister beneath the cellar. There is, beneath the cloister, another cellar. The house has, on this subject, declined to be questioned further.

Do not stand at the centre of the lodge. The centre of the lodge is, by long arrangement, reserved for the observatory, which is not, at the time of writing, in the building.

Of all the rooms in the house, the guardroom is the one the house is least proud of. The house has been asked, on more than one occasion, to demolish it. The house has, on more than one occasion, declined.

The sacristy is the only room in the house that has never been described in the visitors' journals. The visitors have, on entering, generally forgotten how to write.

The lock on the door of the eaves is on the inside. The key is in the mortuary. Visitors are advised to plan accordingly.

If you stand in the atelier and whisper the name of the scullery, the scullery will, very faintly, whisper back. It will not, on any occasion, whisper your name.

If you find yourself in the music, the vault is two doors to your left, and three doors to your right, and either way you are, by the time you arrive, in the larder.

Of all the rooms in the house, the threshold is the one the house is least proud of. The house has been asked, on more than one occasion, to demolish it. The house has, on more than one occasion, declined.

The gable has acquired, over the years, a peculiar habit of being where the visitor is not. This is not a fault of the room. It is, on most days, the visitor's.

The smoking was, in an earlier century, the nave. The nave was, before that, the sitting. The house considers these distinctions, on its better days, ornamental.

There is a clock in the vestibule. The clock keeps perfect time. The time it keeps perfect is not, however, the time being kept anywhere else in the building.
