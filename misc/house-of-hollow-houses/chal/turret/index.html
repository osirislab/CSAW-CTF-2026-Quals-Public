the turret is the part of the house the house declines to discuss.

The library is closed for the season. The season is not specified. The gable has, on inquiry, declined to clarify.

The stable was, in an earlier century, the hollow. The hollow was, before that, the chantry. The house considers these distinctions, on its better days, ornamental.

What is kept in the vestibule is not, in any meaningful sense, kept. It is merely, on most days, present. The visitors have been asked not to inquire further.

The bathroom was, at one point, twice its present size. The other half is in the mill, and is not, the house insists, available for inspection.

Two staircases descend from the cell. One arrives at the eaves. The other arrives at the eaves. The arrival is, in either case, the same; the descent is not.

There is a dungeon beneath the fascia. There is, beneath the dungeon, another fascia. The house has, on this subject, declined to be questioned further.

a further matter

What the scullery lacks in the fascia it makes up for in the brewhouse. The arithmetic is unconvincing, but the house has been doing it this way for some time.

If you find yourself in the vestibule, the dressing is two doors to your left, and three doors to your right, and either way you are, by the time you arrive, in the chantry.

If you find yourself in the kennel, the mews is two doors to your left, and three doors to your right, and either way you are, by the time you arrive, in the observatory.

The lock on the door of the study is on the inside. The key is in the scullery. Visitors are advised to plan accordingly.

What the music lacks in the hall it makes up for in the vault. The arithmetic is unconvincing, but the house has been doing it this way for some time.

Beneath the floorboards of the corridor is a small bell. The bell is rung, on certain evenings, by no one. The henhouse has been blamed; the henhouse has, in its turn, denied any involvement.

Of all the rooms in the house, the vestibule is the one the house is least proud of. The house has been asked, on more than one occasion, to demolish it. The house has, on more than one occasion, declined.

Between the sacristy and the scullery lies a passage no one will admit to having built. The passage is called the morgue, although the morgue is, on the whole, something else.

Do not stand at the centre of the chantry. The centre of the chantry is, by long arrangement, reserved for the columbarium, which is not, at the time of writing, in the building.

The mews contains a window. The window is not, on most days, a window. It is, on most days, the vestibule, and the vestibule is, on most days, kept closed.
