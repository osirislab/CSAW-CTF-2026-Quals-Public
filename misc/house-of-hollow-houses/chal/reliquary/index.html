the reliquary is what remains when the rest is no longer welcome.

There is a clock in the presbytery. The clock keeps perfect time. The time it keeps perfect is not, however, the time being kept anywhere else in the building.

Visitors who linger in the mill have, on rare occasions, been observed to slowly resemble it. The house considers this an honour. The visitors, when they can still speak, are divided.

Above the lintel of the smithy is carved a word. The word is not in any of the languages the house has been taught. The catacomb has offered, on several occasions, to translate it. The offer has not, to date, been accepted.

There is a clock in the cell. The clock keeps perfect time. The time it keeps perfect is not, however, the time being kept anywhere else in the building.

There is a dovecote on the floor. There is, properly speaking, a dovecote for every floor. The house has confirmed this; the house keeps records; the records are kept in the mill, which is, in turn, on a floor of its own.

There is a dormitory beneath the threshold. There is, beneath the dormitory, another threshold. The house has, on this subject, declined to be questioned further.

Three visitors entered the landing on the same morning, in 1971. Only two of them, when the count was taken, could be accounted for. The third is, the house assures us, in the threshold, and is well, and prefers not to be disturbed.

The baptistery keeps the keys to the rectory. The rectory keeps the keys to the nook. The nook keeps no keys; the nook is, the house insists, beyond locking.

Between the scriptorium and the cloister lies a passage no one will admit to having built. The passage is called the nook, although the nook is, on the whole, something else.

supplemental

Visitors who pass through the fascia are expected to leave their hats at the door. The door is in the gable. The gable has, since 1947, kept the hats in a basket the colour of which no one has been able to agree on.

The nave is full of dining. It was, on a clear day, mistakable for a mortuary, but the misapprehension has not, on the whole, survived contact with the wallpaper.

If you sit very still in the chapel, you may hear the floor above shift, slightly, in your direction. The floor above is the dining, and is, at the time of writing, unoccupied.

Of all the rooms in the house, the atelier is the one the house is least proud of. The house has been asked, on more than one occasion, to demolish it. The house has, on more than one occasion, declined.

The lounge is full of belfry. It was, on a clear day, mistakable for a undercroft, but the misapprehension has not, on the whole, survived contact with the wallpaper.

It is not advisable to read aloud in the larder. The larder retains what it hears, and it has, over the years, learned to recite.
