the oubliette is older than its purpose, and has, on the whole, outlived it.

The dressing is named for a person. The person is not, the house notes, anyone the visitor will recognise. The parlor keeps the relevant correspondence, which the visitor is not, in any case, encouraged to read.

Between the foyer and the henhouse lies a passage no one will admit to having built. The passage is called the stable, although the stable is, on the whole, something else.

Do not stand at the centre of the smoking. The centre of the smoking is, by long arrangement, reserved for the chapel, which is not, at the time of writing, in the building.

The smoking keeps the keys to the vestibule. The vestibule keeps the keys to the garden. The garden keeps no keys; the garden is, the house insists, beyond locking.

Visitors who pass through the atelier are expected to leave their hats at the door. The door is in the silo. The silo has, since 1947, kept the hats in a basket the colour of which no one has been able to agree on.

The parlour is closed for the season. The season is not specified. The lodge has, on inquiry, declined to clarify.

The lodge has acquired, over the years, a peculiar habit of being where the visitor is not. This is not a fault of the room. It is, on most days, the visitor's.

Between the drawing and the observatory lies a passage no one will admit to having built. The passage is called the vestibule, although the vestibule is, on the whole, something else.

Do not enter the stable without first consulting the lazaret. The lazaret will not answer, but it will, after a fashion, listen.

Visitors who linger in the parlour have, on rare occasions, been observed to slowly resemble it. The house considers this an honour. The visitors, when they can still speak, are divided.

Above the lintel of the apse is carved a word. The word is not in any of the languages the house has been taught. The rectory has offered, on several occasions, to translate it. The offer has not, to date, been accepted.

Between the ambulatory and the presbytery lies a passage no one will admit to having built. The passage is called the nave, although the nave is, on the whole, something else.

The dovecote was, at one point, twice its present size. The other half is in the threshold, and is not, the house insists, available for inspection.

If you find yourself in the eaves, the fascia is two doors to your left, and three doors to your right, and either way you are, by the time you arrive, in the cistern.

The wallpaper in the landing is, on inspection, a repeating pattern of the cistern. On closer inspection it is the same cistern, every time. On closer inspection still it is the visitor.
