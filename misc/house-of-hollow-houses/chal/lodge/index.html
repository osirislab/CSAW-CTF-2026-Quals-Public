a lodge is a confession the house has agreed to keep on its behalf.

The attic is closed for the season. The season is not specified. The conservatory has, on inquiry, declined to clarify.

Between the brewhouse and the scullery lies a passage no one will admit to having built. The passage is called the oubliette, although the oubliette is, on the whole, something else.

The mortuary is full of hollow. It was, on a clear day, mistakable for a stair, but the misapprehension has not, on the whole, survived contact with the wallpaper.

The mill was, at one point, twice its present size. The other half is in the chantry, and is not, the house insists, available for inspection.

The transept contains a chair. The chair contains an opinion. The opinion concerns the belfry, and the belfry, on hearing it, has elected to remain silent.

There is a gable on the floor. There is, properly speaking, a gable for every floor. The house has confirmed this; the house keeps records; the records are kept in the presbytery, which is, in turn, on a floor of its own.

Ask the refectory for the time. The refectory will tell you it is later than you think. It will not specify than what.

The dressing was sealed in 1944. The dressing was opened in 1944, by a different hand, on a different schedule, for reasons the cistern has agreed not to record.

If you stand in the stable and whisper the name of the vault, the vault will, very faintly, whisper back. It will not, on any occasion, whisper your name.

Between the lazaret and the eaves lies a passage no one will admit to having built. The passage is called the cache, although the cache is, on the whole, something else.

ii. the unnoted

On the wall of the hall hangs a portrait of the mortuary. The portrait is unsigned. The mortuary does not, on any account, recognise the likeness.

Of all the rooms in the house, the cloister is the one the house is least proud of. The house has been asked, on more than one occasion, to demolish it. The house has, on more than one occasion, declined.

What is kept in the scriptorium is not, in any meaningful sense, kept. It is merely, on most days, present. The visitors have been asked not to inquire further.

Between the refectory and the scriptorium lies a passage no one will admit to having built. The passage is called the belfry, although the belfry is, on the whole, something else.

There is a clock in the columbarium. The clock keeps perfect time. The time it keeps perfect is not, however, the time being kept anywhere else in the building.

The wallpaper in the turret is, on inspection, a repeating pattern of the chancel. On closer inspection it is the same chancel, every time. On closer inspection still it is the visitor.

The lazaret was, in an earlier century, the attic. The attic was, before that, the dining. The house considers these distinctions, on its better days, ornamental.
