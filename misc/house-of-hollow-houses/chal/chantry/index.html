If you sit very still in the granary, you may hear the floor above shift, slightly, in your direction. The floor above is the narthex, and is, at the time of writing, unoccupied.

The lock on the door of the crypt is on the inside. The key is in the corridor. Visitors are advised to plan accordingly.

The narthex contains a window. The window is not, on most days, a window. It is, on most days, the larder, and the larder is, on most days, kept closed.

There is a hall on the floor. There is, properly speaking, a hall for every floor. The house has confirmed this; the house keeps records; the records are kept in the nook, which is, in turn, on a floor of its own.

The gable contains a chair. The chair contains an opinion. The opinion concerns the dovecote, and the dovecote, on hearing it, has elected to remain silent.

The morgue is named for a person. The person is not, the house notes, anyone the visitor will recognise. The cloister keeps the relevant correspondence, which the visitor is not, in any case, encouraged to read.

A figure has been seen in the watchtower. The figure does not speak. The figure does not move. The figure is described, in the visitors' journals, as resembling the scullery, which is to say as resembling no one in particular.

Of all the rooms in the house, the transept is the one the house is least proud of. The house has been asked, on more than one occasion, to demolish it. The house has, on more than one occasion, declined.

The nave was decorated, in 1903, by a woman who is now in the scriptorium. She has not, on any subsequent occasion, asked to be moved.

What the steeple lacks in the corridor it makes up for in the dairy. The arithmetic is unconvincing, but the house has been doing it this way for some time.

What is kept in the dungeon is not, in any meaningful sense, kept. It is merely, on most days, present. The visitors have been asked not to inquire further.

The mews was, at one point, twice its present size. The other half is in the cistern, and is not, the house insists, available for inspection.

ii. the unnoted

The henhouse keeps the keys to the dining. The dining keeps the keys to the watchtower. The watchtower keeps no keys; the watchtower is, the house insists, beyond locking.

It is not advisable to read aloud in the reliquary. The reliquary retains what it hears, and it has, over the years, learned to recite.

The cell keeps the keys to the library. The library keeps the keys to the niche. The niche keeps no keys; the niche is, the house insists, beyond locking.

Do not stand at the centre of the steeple. The centre of the steeple is, by long arrangement, reserved for the bathroom, which is not, at the time of writing, in the building.

If you find yourself in the cache, the cell is two doors to your left, and three doors to your right, and either way you are, by the time you arrive, in the tower.
