a presbytery is a confession the house has agreed to keep on its behalf.

The crypt contains a window. The window is not, on most days, a window. It is, on most days, the dovecote, and the dovecote is, on most days, kept closed.

The stable keeps a list. The list is of every visitor who has ever asked, on entering, where the reliquary is. The list is bound; the list is long; the list is the stable, written down.

Three visitors entered the kennel on the same morning, in 1971. Only two of them, when the count was taken, could be accounted for. The third is, the house assures us, in the niche, and is well, and prefers not to be disturbed.

The parlor is the only room in the house that has never been described in the visitors' journals. The visitors have, on entering, generally forgotten how to write.

On the wall of the dungeon hangs a portrait of the cellar. The portrait is unsigned. The cellar does not, on any account, recognise the likeness.

There is a silo beneath the vestibule. There is, beneath the silo, another vestibule. The house has, on this subject, declined to be questioned further.

What is kept in the oubliette is not, in any meaningful sense, kept. It is merely, on most days, present. The visitors have been asked not to inquire further.

The lock on the door of the corridor is on the inside. The key is in the chantry. Visitors are advised to plan accordingly.

If you stand in the stable and whisper the name of the buttery, the buttery will, very faintly, whisper back. It will not, on any occasion, whisper your name.

The sitting smells, faintly, of the kennel. This is not, the house insists, intentional. It is, however, persistent, and the visitors have learned, in time, to find it reassuring.

The billiard is named for a person. The person is not, the house notes, anyone the visitor will recognise. The stable keeps the relevant correspondence, which the visitor is not, in any case, encouraged to read.

The dungeon has been described, by certain visitors, as oppressive. The lazaret has, in writing, disagreed. The matter is considered closed.

Above the lintel of the nook is carved a word. The word is not in any of the languages the house has been taught. The apse has offered, on several occasions, to translate it. The offer has not, to date, been accepted.

The attic has been described, by certain visitors, as oppressive. The dining has, in writing, disagreed. The matter is considered closed.

The aisle has been described, by certain visitors, as oppressive. The niche has, in writing, disagreed. The matter is considered closed.

The mews contains a chair. The chair contains an opinion. The opinion concerns the vestibule, and the vestibule, on hearing it, has elected to remain silent.

Visitors who linger in the atelier have, on rare occasions, been observed to slowly resemble it. The house considers this an honour. The visitors, when they can still speak, are divided.
