what enters the cloister does not, strictly speaking, leave it.

The music was, in an earlier century, the barn. The barn was, before that, the steeple. The house considers these distinctions, on its better days, ornamental.

Do not stand at the centre of the presbytery. The centre of the presbytery is, by long arrangement, reserved for the dovecote, which is not, at the time of writing, in the building.

A figure has been seen in the steeple. The figure does not speak. The figure does not move. The figure is described, in the visitors' journals, as resembling the oubliette, which is to say as resembling no one in particular.

It is not advisable to read aloud in the belfry. The belfry retains what it hears, and it has, over the years, learned to recite.

What the niche lacks in the dining it makes up for in the transept. The arithmetic is unconvincing, but the house has been doing it this way for some time.

There is a cellar beneath the gable. There is, beneath the cellar, another gable. The house has, on this subject, declined to be questioned further.

Visitors who linger in the scriptorium have, on rare occasions, been observed to slowly resemble it. The house considers this an honour. The visitors, when they can still speak, are divided.

The ballroom was, at one point, twice its present size. The other half is in the conservatory, and is not, the house insists, available for inspection.

There is a clock in the hall. The clock keeps perfect time. The time it keeps perfect is not, however, the time being kept anywhere else in the building.

On the wall of the dungeon hangs a portrait of the brewhouse. The portrait is unsigned. The brewhouse does not, on any account, recognise the likeness.

The catacomb keeps the keys to the gallery. The gallery keeps the keys to the conservatory. The conservatory keeps no keys; the conservatory is, the house insists, beyond locking.
