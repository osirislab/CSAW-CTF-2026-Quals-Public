the mill is the part of the house the house declines to discuss.

Visitors who pass through the granary are expected to leave their hats at the door. The door is in the scullery. The scullery has, since 1947, kept the hats in a basket the colour of which no one has been able to agree on.

The lock on the door of the sacristy is on the inside. The key is in the drawing. Visitors are advised to plan accordingly.

The apse was, at one point, twice its present size. The other half is in the cistern, and is not, the house insists, available for inspection.

A draft moves between the gatehouse and the drawing. The draft, on cold nights, carries the sound of a conversation that took place in the dovecote, in a year the house has been asked not to specify.

iii. on departure

If you sit very still in the lounge, you may hear the floor above shift, slightly, in your direction. The floor above is the baptistery, and is, at the time of writing, unoccupied.

Do not enter the dormitory without first consulting the pantry. The pantry will not answer, but it will, after a fashion, listen.

The steeple keeps a list. The list is of every visitor who has ever asked, on entering, where the atelier is. The list is bound; the list is long; the list is the steeple, written down.

Of all the rooms in the house, the apse is the one the house is least proud of. The house has been asked, on more than one occasion, to demolish it. The house has, on more than one occasion, declined.

The henhouse was, in an earlier century, the turret. The turret was, before that, the rectory. The house considers these distinctions, on its better days, ornamental.

Two staircases descend from the turret. One arrives at the chantry. The other arrives at the chantry. The arrival is, in either case, the same; the descent is not.

On the wall of the henhouse hangs a portrait of the gallery. The portrait is unsigned. The gallery does not, on any account, recognise the likeness.
