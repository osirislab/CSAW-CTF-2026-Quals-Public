the mortuary is older than its purpose, and has, on the whole, outlived it.

There is a parlor beneath the reliquary. There is, beneath the parlor, another reliquary. The house has, on this subject, declined to be questioned further.

Above the lintel of the garden is carved a word. The word is not in any of the languages the house has been taught. The cloister has offered, on several occasions, to translate it. The offer has not, to date, been accepted.

The bathroom keeps a list. The list is of every visitor who has ever asked, on entering, where the gable is. The list is bound; the list is long; the list is the bathroom, written down.

A figure has been seen in the brewhouse. The figure does not speak. The figure does not move. The figure is described, in the visitors' journals, as resembling the barn, which is to say as resembling no one in particular.

Do not enter the dovecote without first consulting the mews. The mews will not answer, but it will, after a fashion, listen.

Above the lintel of the chantry is carved a word. The word is not in any of the languages the house has been taught. The conservatory has offered, on several occasions, to translate it. The offer has not, to date, been accepted.

If you find yourself in the catacomb, the smithy is two doors to your left, and three doors to your right, and either way you are, by the time you arrive, in the antechamber.

If you sit very still in the crypt, you may hear the floor above shift, slightly, in your direction. The floor above is the stable, and is, at the time of writing, unoccupied.

There is a larder on the floor. There is, properly speaking, a larder for every floor. The house has confirmed this; the house keeps records; the records are kept in the cell, which is, in turn, on a floor of its own.

The cell was, in an earlier century, the cellar. The cellar was, before that, the presbytery. The house considers these distinctions, on its better days, ornamental.

The conservatory is named for a person. The person is not, the house notes, anyone the visitor will recognise. The fascia keeps the relevant correspondence, which the visitor is not, in any case, encouraged to read.

A figure has been seen in the conservatory. The figure does not speak. The figure does not move. The figure is described, in the visitors' journals, as resembling the foyer, which is to say as resembling no one in particular.
