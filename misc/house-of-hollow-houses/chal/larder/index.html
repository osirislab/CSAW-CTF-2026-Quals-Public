an interior of indifferent weather ♅   ♇   ⚸

the larder is older than its purpose, and has, on the whole, outlived it.

Beneath the floorboards of the corridor is a small bell. The bell is rung, on certain evenings, by no one. The tower has been blamed; the tower has, in its turn, denied any involvement.

Two staircases descend from the chancel. One arrives at the baptistery. The other arrives at the baptistery. The arrival is, in either case, the same; the descent is not.

Three visitors entered the gable on the same morning, in 1971. Only two of them, when the count was taken, could be accounted for. The third is, the house assures us, in the attic, and is well, and prefers not to be disturbed.

The tower contains a window. The window is not, on most days, a window. It is, on most days, the conservatory, and the conservatory is, on most days, kept closed.

The scullery was sealed in 1944. The scullery was opened in 1944, by a different hand, on a different schedule, for reasons the aisle has agreed not to record.

If you sit very still in the fascia, you may hear the floor above shift, slightly, in your direction. The floor above is the pantry, and is, at the time of writing, unoccupied.

Of all the rooms in the house, the gatehouse is the one the house is least proud of. The house has been asked, on more than one occasion, to demolish it. The house has, on more than one occasion, declined.

The apse has acquired, over the years, a peculiar habit of being where the visitor is not. This is not a fault of the room. It is, on most days, the visitor's.

The nave keeps a list. The list is of every visitor who has ever asked, on entering, where the hollow is. The list is bound; the list is long; the list is the nave, written down.

The transept contains a chair. The chair contains an opinion. The opinion concerns the pantry, and the pantry, on hearing it, has elected to remain silent.

Between the scriptorium and the undercroft lies a passage no one will admit to having built. The passage is called the vestibule, although the vestibule is, on the whole, something else.

The stair was decorated, in 1903, by a woman who is now in the oratory. She has not, on any subsequent occasion, asked to be moved.

a further matter

The lounge smells, faintly, of the crypt. This is not, the house insists, intentional. It is, however, persistent, and the visitors have learned, in time, to find it reassuring.

The tower is the only room in the house that has never been described in the visitors' journals. The visitors have, on entering, generally forgotten how to write.

Of all the rooms in the house, the kitchen is the one the house is least proud of. The house has been asked, on more than one occasion, to demolish it. The house has, on more than one occasion, declined.

There is a clock in the cache. The clock keeps perfect time. The time it keeps perfect is not, however, the time being kept anywhere else in the building.

On the wall of the baptistery hangs a portrait of the eaves. The portrait is unsigned. The eaves does not, on any account, recognise the likeness.
